1. Why invest?

Saving is useful for money you may need soon. Investing is designed for longer-term goals because your money has time to grow—but investment values can rise and fall. Retirement is one of the biggest long-term goals most people will ever fund.

You are not investing just to “have investments.” You are trying to build a future source of income for the years when you are no longer earning a paycheck from work.
Quick thought: Why might retirement be a reason to invest instead of keeping all long-term money as cash?

6. Social Security: what is it, and is it enough?

Workers and employers help fund Social Security through payroll taxes. In 2026, employees pay 6.2% Social Security tax on covered wages up to the annual taxable maximum. The paycheck may show this as Social Security, OASDI, or part of FICA. Medicare payroll tax is separate.

If you earn per month at a job, about would be the employee Social Security tax for that month (assuming all of those wages are covered and below the annual cap).
Under-the-table work matters here: Social Security benefits are based on covered earnings in your work record. If earnings are not reported and Social Security taxes are not paid on them, those earnings generally do not build your Social Security earnings record. SSA uses a worker's highest 35 years of indexed covered earnings in its retirement-benefit calculation.

The estimated average retired-worker benefit for January 2026 was about $2,071/month. That is useful context—not a prediction of your future benefit.

For this simulator, Social Security is fixed at $2,071/month. That is the January 2026 estimated average retired-worker benefit and gives everyone the same classroom illustration. Your actual future benefit would depend on your covered earnings record and other factors.
Sources: Social Security Administration 2026 COLA Fact Sheet; SSA retirement benefit calculation materials. A real future estimate should use your own earnings record through SSA's calculators.

7. What is a pension?

A defined-benefit pension is an employer retirement plan that promises a benefit according to the plan's rules—often connected to salary and years of service. Many jobs do not offer one, and students should not assume their future job will.

Do you still need to invest if you expect a pension? What did adding or removing a pension do to your remaining paycheck gap?

8. What if I don't have a pension? 401(k) and Roth IRA

401(k)

A retirement account offered through an employer. Contributions can come directly from your paycheck. Many traditional 401(k) contributions reduce taxable income now and are generally taxed when withdrawn later.

Big feature: an employer may match part of what you contribute.

Roth IRA

An individual retirement account you open yourself. Contributions are made with money that has already been taxed; qualified withdrawals in retirement can be tax-free.

Big question: pay income tax on the money now or later?

Account ≠ investment. Opening a 401(k) or Roth IRA does not automatically mean the money is invested. The account holds investments such as funds. The investment growth is what helps build future income.
Employer retirement benefits are not guaranteed. Some employers offer a 401(k), some offer a 401(k) with a match, and some may offer no workplace retirement account at all. If your employer does not offer a retirement account, you can still save for retirement on your own—for example, by opening an IRA such as a Roth IRA, if you are eligible. A Roth IRA does not come with an employer match.
Employer match: For our classroom example, an employer that offers a match will match contributions up to 3% of salary. Actual employers use different formulas, and many offer no match. Vesting means the rules for when employer-contributed money becomes fully yours; your own contributions remain yours.
